Inspecting Composite Structures

Section 1 of 7

Learning outcomes

This short course looks at why composite parts of an aircraft are harder to inspect than metal ones, what kinds of damage they suffer, and how that damage is found. It uses the aircraft radome as a worked example.

By the end, you should be able to:

  • Explain the main challenge of inspecting composite structures compared with metal structures.
  • Tell apart five common types of composite damage (erosion, impact, moisture intrusion, lightning strike and delamination) and the inspection methods used for each.
  • Explain the reasoning behind the instructions given for taking airframe components apart and putting them back together.

Section 2 of 7

Challenges of composite inspection

Metal and composite structures fail in very different ways, and that changes how you inspect them. When metal is damaged, it usually shows. Corrosion leaves visible marks on the surface, and cracks can often be seen with the naked eye.

Composite damage is much more likely to stay hidden. Many composite parts are built as a "sandwich": two thin outer skins bonded to a lighter core in between. A defect can sit inside the core, or on the far skin, with nothing showing on the surface you are looking at.

In practice

A clean, undamaged-looking outer surface on a composite part does not prove the part is sound. A visual check is only the start, and other inspection methods are needed to find damage below the surface.

Key takeaways

  • Damage in metal usually leaves visible signs on the outside.
  • Damage in composites can stay concealed inside the structure.

Quick check

Why are composite structures harder to inspect than metal ones?

Unlike metal, composite damage often remains concealed inside the structure.

Section 3 of 7

What damages composites

Composite structures on an aircraft are typically damaged in three ways:

  • Erosion from rain or hailstones wearing away the surface.
  • Lightning strikes or static discharges.
  • Impact from bird strikes, from foreign object damage (FOD) such as debris thrown up from the runway, or from accidents with ground vehicles.

The trouble is that the visible signs can be small. Serious damage may show up only as cracks, crazing (a network of fine surface cracks), stains or scuffs in the paintwork. Any of these should lead to a closer examination for defects below the surface.

An impact can also travel. The shock can be passed on through the structure to the points where the part is attached, so those attachment points and the parts next to them should be checked for secondary damage too.

In practice

A scuff on the paint after a bird strike is not just cosmetic. Treat it as a reason to look beneath the surface, and to check the attachment points as well.

Key takeaways

  • The main causes of damage are erosion, lightning or static discharge, and impact.
  • Small marks in the paint can hide larger defects beneath the surface.
  • Check attachment points for secondary damage passed on by the impact.

Quick check

You find light scuffs in the paint of a composite panel after a bird strike. What should you do?

Visible marks may be the only sign of sub-surface damage, so further examination is required.

Besides the impact area itself, where else should you look for damage?

The shock can be passed on to attachment points, causing secondary damage there.

Section 4 of 7

Moisture and the freeze-thaw effect

Any break in the surface of a composite laminate (layers of material bonded together) or its honeycomb core gives moisture a way in. Once water is inside, it keeps causing damage during normal flight operations.

This happens through a repeating freeze-thaw cycle:

  1. Water gets trapped in a crack.
  2. At altitude the water freezes. Water expands by about 9% when it freezes, which pushes on the crack and can open it further.
  3. Back on the ground the ice melts, and the water seeps deeper into the now larger crack.

Each flight repeats the cycle, so the bonded structure is gradually destroyed from the inside.

In practice

If you suspect moisture has got in through a puncture in the skin, investigate how far the moisture has spread through the structure, not just the puncture itself.

Key takeaways

  • Any surface damage can let moisture into the structure.
  • Repeated freezing and thawing makes cracks grow with each flight.
  • When moisture is suspected, check how far it has been absorbed.

Quick check

Why does trapped water make a crack worse?

Freezing water expands, stressing the crack; when it melts it penetrates further.

Moisture may have entered through a skin puncture. What should the investigation check?

The investigation should establish the extent of moisture absorption.

Section 5 of 7

Radome damage

The radome is the composite nose cone that covers an aircraft's radar. Because it faces forward into the weather, it takes a lot of punishment. A transport aircraft's radome lasts about three years on average.

The common types of radome damage are:

  • Lightning strikes
  • Erosion
  • Impact from bird strikes
  • Delamination: the internal layers separating from each other
  • Water ingression: water collecting between the layers

In practice

The cost adds up. A large airline can spend $100,000 to $250,000 a year repairing radomes, and most of that is due to lightning strikes and other thunderstorm damage.

Key takeaways

  • A transport aircraft radome lasts about three years on average.
  • Its common damage types are lightning, erosion, bird strike, delamination and water ingression.
  • Lightning and thunderstorms cause most radome repairs.

Quick check

What is delamination?

Delamination is separation of internal layers; water between layers is water ingression.

What causes most radome repairs at a large airline?

Most radome repair cost is due to lightning strikes and thunderstorm-related activity.

Section 6 of 7

Radome inspection requirements

A radome is typically made of glass fibre reinforced plastic (GFRP) built around a honeycomb core. Metal lightning diverter strips are fitted along its outside. They give the electric current from a lightning strike a safe path to the fuselage, the main body of the aircraft.

Inspection starts with a visual check to judge the radome's condition and whether it is fit for service. During this check, pay particular attention to:

  • fasteners
  • paintwork
  • the lightning diverter strips
  • any irregularities on the surface

If the damage goes beyond the limits given in the reference documentation, the radome must be repaired or replaced with a serviceable one.

Key takeaways

  • Radomes are usually GFRP with a honeycomb core and lightning diverter strips.
  • A visual inspection is always the first step.
  • Damage beyond reference limits means repair or replacement.

Quick check

What do lightning diverter strips do?

Diverter strips let current from a lightning strike flow to the fuselage.

Radome damage is found to be beyond the reference limits. What happens next?

Any damage beyond reference limits must be repaired, or the radome replaced.

Section 7 of 7

Wrap-up

  • Composite damage often hides below the surface, so a clean surface is not proof of a sound part.
  • Erosion, lightning and impact are the main causes, and small paint marks can hide bigger problems.
  • Moisture that gets in is made worse by freeze-thaw cycles during flight.
  • Radomes face lightning, erosion, bird strikes, delamination and water ingression.
  • Radome inspection starts visually, and damage beyond limits means repair or replacement.
